# Miles Davis — *Kind of Blue* (1959)

## Overview

***Kind of Blue*** is a studio album by American jazz musician **Miles Davis**, released on August 17, 1959, by Columbia Records. It stands as one of the most important and influential albums in the history of jazz — often cited as **the greatest jazz album ever recorded**.

The album features Davis's legendary sextet: saxophonists **John Coltrane** and **Julian "Cannonball" Adderley**, pianist **Bill Evans**, bassist **Paul Chambers**, drummer **Jimmy Cobb**, and pianist **Wynton Kelly** (who replaced Evans on one track).

Recorded at Columbia's 30th Street Studio in New York City over two sessions (March 2 and April 22, 1959), the album runs approximately **45 minutes** in length.

---

## The Story — How It All Began

### Miles Davis's Musical Evolution

By late 1958, Miles Davis was at the height of his power as one of the most acclaimed and profitable musicians in the jazz world. His band featured:

- **Paul Chambers** (bass) — with the group since 1955
- **Cannonball Adderley** (alto sax) — joined late 1957
- **John Coltrane** (tenor sax) — returned early 1958
- **Jimmy Cobb** (drums) — replaced Philly Joe Jones in May 1958
- **Wynton Kelly** (piano) — replaced Bill Evans in November 1958

But Davis was growing **dissatisfied with bebop** — the complex chord changes and rapid-fire improvisation that had dominated jazz for years. He felt it was constraining creativity rather than liberating it.

### The Modal Revolution

Influenced by **George Russell's *Lydian Chromatic Concept of Tonal Organization*** (1953), Davis began experimenting with **modal composition** — using scales and modes as the basis for improvisation rather than complex chord changes.

He implemented his first modal composition with the title track of his 1958 album ***Milestones***. The results were revolutionary:
- Musicians had more freedom to explore melodic ideas
- Less pressure from rapid chord changes
- More space for creativity and emotion

Satisfied with these results, Davis decided to create an **entire album** based on modality — *Kind of Blue*.

### The "No Rehearsal" Legend

Here's where the story gets fascinating:

**Davis called for almost no rehearsal.** The musicians had little idea what they were about to record. As Bill Evans described in the original liner notes:

> Davis had only given the band sketches of scales and melody lines on which to improvise.

This persistent legend — that the entire album was recorded in one pass — is **partially true**: only "Flamenco Sketches" yielded a complete take on the first try. The rest required multiple takes.

The genius was in the **minimal preparation** — Davis gave them just enough direction to create something spontaneous and alive, capturing the moment of pure musical discovery.

## The All-Star Lineup

Davis assembled what many consider the **greatest jazz ensemble ever recorded**:

- **Miles Davis** — trumpet (the bandleader, composer, visionary)
- **John Coltrane** — tenor saxophone (at his peak, just before his spiritual transformation)
- **Julian "Cannonball" Adderley** — alto saxophone (fresh from his own hit "Work Song")
- **Bill Evans** — piano (in his prime, known for lyrical, introspective playing)
- **Wynton Kelly** — piano on "Freddie Freeloader" (replaced Evans for this track)
- **Paul Chambers** — double bass (rock-solid timekeeper, Davis's constant companion)
- **Jimmy Cobb** — drums (subtle, supportive, perfect time feel)

## The Modal Concept Explained

### What Is "Modal Jazz"?

Traditional bebop relied on complex chord progressions that changed rapidly — musicians had to navigate these changes while improvising. **Modal jazz** flipped this approach:

- Instead of complex chord changes, musicians worked within **scales and modes**
- Each musician was given a set of scales defining the parameters of their improvisation
- This gave them **more freedom and space** to hear things
- As Davis stated in 1958: *"No chords... gives you a lot more freedom and space to hear things. When you go this way, you can go on forever. You don't have to worry about changes and you can do more with the melody line."*

### Bill Evans's Contribution

Bill Evans wrote in the LP liner notes: **"Miles conceived these settings only hours before the recording dates."**

Many scholars and fans believe that **Bill Evans actually wrote part or all of "Blue in Green" and "Flamenco Sketches"** — though Davis is credited as sole composer on the original release. Evans assumed co-credit with Davis for "Blue in Green" when recording it on his *Portrait in Jazz* album, and the Davis estate acknowledged Evans' authorship in 2002.

## Recording Details

- **Date:** March 2 and April 22, 1959 (two sessions)
- **Location:** Columbia's 30th Street Studio, New York City
- **Format:** Three-track tape
- **Producer:** Irving Townsend (Columbia staff producer) — though Teo Macero has incorrectly received partial or full credit. Davis's subsequent producer Teo Macero has incorrectly received partial or full credit. "In the case of *Kind of Blue* there were two producers: Teo Macero and Irving Townsend," said jazz historian Eric Nisenson. "Macero's role, however, was clearly that of an apprentice and observer." The original LP did not credit a producer.
- **Photography:** Robert Freeman

The recording sessions were remarkably short, suggesting Davis had carefully planned the arrangements and trusted his musicians to deliver without extensive rehearsal.

## Reception & Critical Acclaim

***Kind of Blue*** has received **extraordinary critical acclaim** and remains one of the most studied and performed albums in jazz history:

### Critical Reception

- **AllMusic** — "an album that towers above its peers, a record generally considered as the definitive jazz album"
- **Rolling Stone** — ranked #12 on their list of the "500 Greatest Albums of All Time" (repositioned at #31 in the 2020 revision)
- **Library of Congress** — among the first fifty recordings selected for inclusion in the National Recording Registry as "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant" (2002)

### Influence Beyond Jazz

The album's influence has reached far beyond jazz:

- **Guitarist Duane Allman** of The Allman Brothers Band said his soloing "comes from Miles and Coltrane, and particularly *Kind of Blue*."
- **Pink Floyd keyboardist Richard Wright** said the chord progressions influenced the structure of the introductory chords to "Breathe" on *The Dark Side of the Moon*.
- **Producer Quincy Jones** wrote: "That [*Kind of Blue*] will always be my music, man. I play *Kind of Blue* every day—it's my orange juice. It still sounds like it was made yesterday."
- **Renowned hip-hop artist Q-Tip** reaffirmed the album's reputation: "It's like the Bible—you just have one in your house."

## Legacy & Significance

### Why It Matters

1. **The Modal Revolution:** *Kind of Blue* essentially created modal jazz — a genre that would influence countless musicians across decades and genres.

2. **Perfect Timing:** Davis captured his band at their absolute peak, creating something timeless that transcended the era.

3. **Minimalism as Genius:** The "no rehearsal" approach became legendary — it showed that great music comes from trust, preparation, and capturing the moment rather than over-rehearsing.

4. **Cultural Impact:** The album influenced rock musicians, pop artists, and even hip-hop producers. Its chord progressions and melodic approach became foundational across popular music.

5. **Enduring Relevance:** Despite being recorded in 1959, the album continues to inspire new generations of musicians and listeners.

### Davis in Retrospect

Late in his life, Davis repeatedly disregarded his earlier work:

> *"So What" or Kind of Blue*, they were done in that era, the right hour, the right day, and it happened. It's over .... I have no feel for it anymore—it's more like warmed-over turkey."

When Shirley Horn insisted in 1990 that Davis reconsider playing the gentle ballads and modal tunes of his *Kind of Blue* period, he demurred: **"Nah, it hurts my lip."**

This attitude — dismissing his own masterpiece — is both fascinating and tragic. It speaks to the creative ego of a genius who couldn't believe he'd created something so perfect.

## Sales & Certifications

Between 1991 (when Nielsen SoundScan started tracking sales) and 2016, the album sold **3.6 million copies** in the US. In 2019, the RIAA certified the album **quintuple platinum**, representing five million album-equivalent units.

It remained the **highest-certified jazz album** until August 2026, when Vince Guaraldi's *A Charlie Brown Christmas* was certified 6x platinum.

## Discographic Context

*Kind of Blue* appears in Miles Davis's discography alongside other landmark albums:
- ***Milestones*** (1958) — his first modal album
- ***Bitches Brew*** (1970) — his fusion masterpiece, also his best-selling record

It also features prominently in the discographies of **John Coltrane**, **Bill Evans**, and **Julian Adderley**.

## Fun Facts

- The album is a **note-for-note reproduction** basis for the 2014 album *Blue* by Mostly Other People Do the Killing.
- **Wynton Kelly** replaced Bill Evans on "Freddie Freeloader" — Evans wanted to play something more upbeat, while Kelly brought a different energy.
- Davis's producer Irving Townsend was a Columbia staff member who had worked with Davis before. Teo Macero, his later producer, has incorrectly received partial or full credit for *Kind of Blue*.
- The album's cover art features an abstract design by **Robert Freeman** — not the famous "lonely hearts" collage that would come with Sgt. Pepper.
- Miles Davis called modal composition **"a return to melody"** — he felt that complex chord changes were getting in the way of beautiful melodic expression.

## Summary

***Kind of Blue*** is arguably the most important album in jazz history — and one of the most important albums across all of music. Recorded with minimal preparation, featuring an all-star lineup at their absolute peak, it created a new approach to jazz improvisation that would influence generations of musicians across genres.

The album's genius lies in its simplicity — modal composition gave musicians freedom rather than constraints, capturing the moment of pure musical discovery. Davis's "no rehearsal" approach became legendary, showing that great music comes from trust and preparation rather than over-rehearsing.

Decades later, Quincy Jones still plays it daily: **"That [*Kind of Blue*] will always be my music, man... It still sounds like it was made yesterday."**

The album captures a perfect moment in music history — when innovation and tradition coexisted, when minimalism yielded maximum impact, and when Miles Davis proved he was not just the greatest trumpeter of his generation, but a visionary who changed the course of music forever.
